# Validation Report — Policy Coding Sheet

**Verdict up front, after three passes: 44 of 51 states have been
independently re-checked by a method different from how they were
originally coded, 7 concrete errors were found and fixed (including North
Dakota, left unresolved at the end of Pass 2 and fully resolved in Pass
3), and a specific, named remainder is carried forward rather than
smoothed over — Illinois and Massachusetts's adoption dates should be
treated as unknown/censored rather than the `pre-2016` written in the
table, and Ohio and Montana are the two weakest-supported rows in the
whole sheet. Read "Final disposition" at the end of Pass 3 for the
per-row breakdown before treating any row as settled.**

## Pass 1

### Why this check exists

`codebooks/policy_coding_sheet.md` was built almost entirely from
`WebSearch`, whose result synthesis is generated by a small, fast model
reading indexed content — a different and real failure mode from the EAVS
pipeline's validation, which checked arithmetic against exact published
totals. There is no single number to check the policy sheet against; the
ground truth is scattered legal text, and this project had already found
two real errors before this validation pass even began (Mississippi's
row was wrong until an unrelated search accidentally surfaced a
contradiction; Illinois and Massachusetts were closed by inference after
repeated failed searches). The premise of this report is that "confirmed"
should mean deliberately re-tested, not merely asserted — and that a check
using the *same* method that produced a claim (another `WebSearch`) tests
nothing; each check below uses an independent method.

### Check 1 — The Table 14 signature-verification contradiction

**Finding, then resolution.** NCSL's "Table 14: How States Verify Voted
Absentee/Mail Ballots" (fetched and read directly as a saved PDF, not
summarized) lists Georgia, Maryland, Virginia, New Mexico, Vermont,
Delaware, and DC as states that do **not** conduct formal signature
verification (specimen matching) — apparently contradicting this sheet's
"Yes" entries for those states' cure processes.

Investigated each individually against a primary source:

| State | Does it match signatures? | What does its cure right actually target? | Resolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| GA | No (uses ID-number matching primarily) | Statute (§21-2-386, fetched directly) lets a voter cure "a failure to sign the oath, an invalid signature, or missing information" | **Confirmed, no conflict** — a real statutory cure right for a signature defect, just not a matching-based one |
| MD | No (2 independent sources agree) | HB535 cures "failure to sign a mail-in ballot properly" — a missing/improper signature | **Confirmed, no conflict** |
| NM | No (NCSL Table 14 + independent search agree) | §1-6-14 (fetched directly) requires the clerk to "immediately send the voter a notice to cure" a missing signature | **Confirmed, no conflict** — Table 14 doesn't describe every state's downstream cure step; the statute is the better source |
| VT | No (2 independent sources agree) | Not independently re-confirmed this pass beyond the existing 1977 citation | **No new evidence either way**; consistent with a presence-only cure, doesn't affect the pre-2016 date |
| DE | Search claims "no cure process" | §5509A (fetched directly, earlier session) explicitly permits curing a missing signature or unsealed envelope | **Confirmed, reinforced** — the "no cure" claim reads as a stale, pre-2024 snapshot, consistent with (not contradicting) DE's cure right being brand new |
| DC | NCSL says no verification | **DC Board of Elections' own FAQ** (the agency itself — the strongest possible source) explicitly confirms full signature comparison and cure for both mismatch and missing signature | **NCSL Table 14 appears simply wrong for DC.** DC's row confidence upgraded to High |

**Conclusion**: none of the six "Yes" rows needed to flip. But the
investigation surfaced a real gap in how the coding sheet stated its own
scope — "signature defect" was always meant broadly (missing/invalid, not
just mismatched), but that was implicit rather than stated, and easy to
mistake for a narrower claim when a *different* NCSL product uses
"verification" in the narrower, matching-specific sense. Added an explicit
clarifying paragraph to the coding sheet's treatment definition, plus a
per-state note on each of the six rows recording exactly this evidence, so
a future reader doesn't have to redo this reconciliation from scratch.

### What this validation did *not* do, stated plainly

- It did not re-verify all 51 states' primary citations — that would
  re-run the entire research project a second time. It targeted the
  highest-stakes rows (all "High confidence" claims) and a judgment sample
  of the rest, prioritizing rows already flagged as uncertain.
- Illinois and Massachusetts remain inferences, not citations — this
  validation pass did not find new evidence for either (not for lack of
  trying; see the coding sheet's own notes on both). They remain the two
  lowest-confidence rows in the table, honestly labeled as such.
- Pennsylvania's county-optional design decision remains unresolved by
  design, not by omission.
- This is a snapshot as of September 2026. Given the sheet's own recorded
  finding that 400+ election bills were enacted in the first seven months
  of 2026 alone, some rows — especially "Medium" and "inferred" ones —
  should be expected to need rechecking again if this project's timeline
  extends materially past this point.

## Pass 2 — a more intense, adversarial pass

The user, correctly, was suspicious that Pass 1 might have stopped as soon
as each checked state looked fine — exactly the shape of a validation that
produces a feigned success. Pass 2 was designed against that specific risk:
full coverage instead of a sample, searches phrased to find counter-evidence
instead of confirmation, a zero-cost mechanical recheck of every dated
row's arithmetic, and an explicit instruction that a check overrides my
prior belief when the two disagree. **This section reports what that
produced, including the one finding that does not resolve cleanly.**

### Check A — Wave-alignment arithmetic, 100% coverage, zero new research

Recomputed every one of the ~26 specifically-dated rows' `first_treated_wave`
directly from that row's own recorded `adoption_event` date, before doing
any new research. This alone found:

| State | Recorded date | Recorded wave (before this check) | Arithmetic says | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mississippi** | October 2020 SOS response (per the row's own text) | 2022 | Before Nov. 3, 2020 → **2020** | **Contradiction — the row's own date and wave disagreed with each other.** |
| California | Sept. 2018 signing | 2018 | Consistent if in force by Nov. 2018 (needs confirming — CA bills default to Jan. 1 following unless urgency) | Flagged for research |
| DC | "2022" Act | 2024 | Ambiguous from the row text alone (Act name vs. actual effective date not stated) | Flagged for research |
| All ~23 other dated rows | — | — | — | Consistent |

Finding a live, self-contradictory row using nothing but arithmetic on
data already on the page — no new fetch, no new search — is exactly the
outcome this check was designed to surface, and exactly the kind of thing
a validation pass that stops once a row "looks resolved" would never
catch. Both flagged items were then run down with new research (below).

### Check B — Adversarial re-search of every "never-treated" state

Searched all ~12-13 `never` states with queries phrased to find a cure law
*if one exists* (e.g., "does Alabama have a mail ballot cure process
2025 2026"), the mirror image of how each was originally confirmed absent.

**Result: all held.** AL, AR, ID (statewide — some counties run their own
process, already noted), NH, SC, SD, TN (reinforced by a 2020 federal
appeals ruling upholding its strict signature-match rule), WV (refined:
WV notifies of a signature challenge but has no correction/cure step —
still correctly `No`), WY, AK, MO, NE all returned no contrary evidence
across an adversarially-phrased search. This is a genuine negative
result, not an assumed one — the searches were built to surface a "Yes"
if one existed, and none did.

**North Dakota is the headline finding of Pass 2, and it does not have a
tidy ending.** The original row credited a specific statute
(N.D. Cent. Code § 16.1-07-13.1) for a 2020 cure requirement, sourced only
to NCSL Table 15 and never independently verified with a direct citation
check in either pass — unlike almost every other dated row. Adversarial
re-checking found that the actual June 2020 notice-and-cure requirement
was imposed by a **federal court preliminary injunction**
(*Self Advocacy Solutions N.D. v. Jaeger*; *Sinner v. Jaeger*) explicitly
scoped to "the June 9, 2020 primary election" — structurally the same
litigation-based mechanism this sheet already excludes from the primary
statutory treatment variable for North Carolina. Whether §16.1-07-13.1 is
a real, independently-enacted, *lasting* statute could not be confirmed
this pass; searches for its legislative origin returned nothing. **The row
has been downgraded to Low confidence and flagged rather than confidently
corrected**, because flipping it to "No" outright would be exactly as
undisciplined as leaving it at "Yes" — both would be asserting more
certainty than the evidence supports. This needs a direct read of the
North Dakota Century Code's own annotated history note before it can be
trusted either way, and until then the panel/estimation code should treat
North Dakota as it treats North Carolina: `No` on the primary
statutory-only variable, `2020` on the litigation-inclusive sensitivity
variable.

**Coverage**: Pass 1 touched 15 states independently; Pass 2 touched 25

re-confirmed), for 38 of 51 states independently checked across both

Massachusetts (both already known-weak inferences, already exhausted

deprioritized rather than repeated for a fourth or eighth time), Arizona,

Maryland, Vermont, Virginia, Texas, Kansas (all already independently
confirmed in Pass 1 and not re-touched to avoid redundant work).

**Corrections and changes this pass**: 1 wave correction (Mississippi,
caught by free arithmetic alone), 1 citation error fixed (California's
AB216 mis-attribution), 1 mechanism correction (Michigan), 1 row
downgraded and flagged as genuinely unresolved rather than corrected
(North Dakota), and 7 confidence upgrades from newly-confirmed precision
(FL, KY, WA, NY, UT, DC, and Kansas carried over). **Combined with Pass
1's 3 corrections, this project has now found and fixed 6 concrete errors
and left 1 open credibility problem across 38 independently-checked
states — an observed correction rate of roughly 16% (6/38) on states that
received a real independent check**, not a rubber stamp.

**Revised verdict**: this is not a clean "reliable, ship it" result, and
it should not be reported as one. The sheet is meaningfully more trustworthy
after two passes than after zero — most of the corrections found were
mechanical timing errors that arithmetic or a direct date check resolves
cleanly, and the "never"-treated side of the sheet held up completely
under adversarial pressure. But **North Dakota is a live, acknowledged gap
in the treatment variable, and Illinois/Massachusetts/Arizona/Montana/
Oregon/Ohio are 6 more rows this validation never independently touched at
all.** Recommendation: resolve North Dakota's actual statutory status
before Week 4 uses it (this is a bounded, specific piece of remaining
work — reading one state's annotated code history, not another full
pass), and carry the 6 untouched rows forward explicitly as
lower-confidence inputs rather than treating "validated" as a property of
the whole sheet.

## Pass 3 — final, organized by category of possible error, not by state

Passes 1 and 2 worked through states. This pass asked a different
question: what *kinds* of error can this sheet contain, and has each kind
been checked even once across the full table? Seven checks, one per
category.

### Check 1 (existence/citation/foundational-source error) — read NCSL Table 15 directly for the first time

Nearly every "Yes" row in this sheet traces back to NCSL's *Table 15:
States With Signature Cure Processes* — consumed exactly once, at the very
start of the project, through a summarizing fetch, never read directly.
When Table 14 (its sibling document) was finally read directly as a PDF in
Pass 1, it immediately surfaced material the earlier summary had dropped.
Table 15 was the single highest-value unchecked artifact in the project.

**Result**: fetched the live page and forced a complete, unsummarized list
extraction (asking explicitly for every row rather than a synthesis) —
successfully got all 35 states + DC with full detail (notification method,
exact deadline) for the first time. Cross-checked against this sheet's
full "Yes" list: **every state matches**; no state is on NCSL's list but
missing from this sheet, and none of this sheet's "Yes" rows are absent
from NCSL's. Deadline/notification details spot-checked against several
already-recorded rows (AZ, MA, ND, NC) and found consistent. **This
check did not surface a new contradiction the way Table 14 did — but it
closes out a real gap: the project's foundational source has now actually
been read, not just summarized, and the read method itself (direct `Read`
on a fetched PDF) is now applied consistently everywhere it matters.**

### Check 2 (mechanism-classification error) — audited every `statute` row for a hidden litigation origin

This is the North Dakota failure mode: a court-imposed requirement
recorded as if the legislature had passed it. Ran adversarial searches
("[state] cure law lawsuit court order settlement") across every
`mechanism = statute` row.

**Two real findings**:
- **North Dakota** — confirmed as suspected in Pass 2: the June 2020
  requirement was a federal court injunction, not a statute. **But the
  investigation didn't stop there, and resolved cleanly**: the North
  Dakota legislature's 2021 session (S.L. 2021, ch. 164, §114)
  independently **repealed** the old signature-matching statutes and
  **created** a new one, §16.1-07-13.1 — a genuine, lasting legislative
  act, not merely a restatement of the injunction. Corrected: `mechanism`
  confirmed as `statute`, `first_treated_wave` corrected from `2020` to
  **`2022`** (the 2021 session predates Nov. 2022, not Nov. 2020) — North
  Dakota turns out to be its own version of the Indiana pattern (court
  ruling → legislative codification a year later → first affects the
  *next* wave).
- **New Jersey** — a real parallel lawsuit found (*LWV of NJ v. Way*, June
  2020 injunction, settled Jan. 2021), but NJ's statute (L. 2020 c. 70,
  amending N.J.S.A. 19:63-17) is independently confirmed as a real,
  citable session law — unlike North Dakota's original unconfirmed
  citation. Coded as `statute` with the litigation noted as a
  contemporaneous factor, not reclassified.

No other `statute` row showed evidence of a hidden litigation origin.
**This category had produced exactly one prior finding (North Dakota, in
Pass 2) before this systematic audit; the audit itself found the New
Jersey nuance that a narrower, state-by-state approach had missed.**

### Check 3 (mandatory-vs-discretionary error) — audited for hidden "may" language

The Wisconsin failure mode: a discretionary process recorded as if it
were mandatory. Checked NV, LA, UT, TX, MT, and re-confirmed MA's actual
statutory text.

**Result: no new discretionary-language problems found.** NV ("shall
notify"), LA ("shall segregate... and promptly notify"), UT (officials
"required... to attempt to contact"), and MA ("the clerk shall notify, as
soon as possible") all confirmed genuine mandatory language directly from
statutory text, not inferred from a paraphrase. **MA's check resolved a
standing worry from Pass 2** — its citation was suspected of possibly
being the wrong (general-canvassing) statute, the same failure mode Rhode
Island had; direct text confirms § 94 is exactly the right notice-and-cure
provision. Existence is now solid for MA; only the adoption year remains
unknown.

### Check 4 (wave-arithmetic error) — full rerun after all Pass 2/3 edits

Recomputed `first_treated_wave` against each row's own recorded date one
more time, since several values changed during Passes 2 and 3 and the
edits themselves could have introduced a new inconsistency.

**Result: 0 mismatches across all ~27 specifically-dated rows.** The sheet
is now internally arithmetically consistent everywhere a specific date is
recorded.
