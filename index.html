Null result · Mail ballot rejection · 2014–2024

A cure that doesn't change the count

A growing number of states now let a voter fix, or “cure,” a mail ballot flagged for a mismatched signature instead of having it thrown out. This project built a jurisdiction-year panel from six waves of the federal government's own election-administration survey and looked for the drop in rejection rates that should follow. Three estimators — built to fail in different directions — agree it isn't there.

The question

A mail ballot can fail for clerical reasons that have nothing to do with eligibility: it arrives late, it's missing a witness signature, or — the case that generates the most litigation — the signature on the return envelope doesn't look enough like the one on file. An election worker compares two scribbles, decides they don't match, and the ballot is set aside.

A signature-cure requirement is the direct fix: the jurisdiction has to notify the voter and give them a window, typically a few days after the election, to confirm their identity so the ballot still counts. It's cheap, largely uncontroversial in principle, and has spread on a staggered timeline across states. If rejection is mostly a paperwork problem, curing should shrink it.

So does it? This analysis matches each state's jurisdiction-year rejection rate against the year its cure requirement first governed an election, and estimates the effect three different ways — a naive two-way fixed-effects regression and two estimators built specifically to correct for that regression's known bias under staggered treatment timing.

What a null result is

Not “nothing was found.” A null result here means: whatever the true effect is, all three estimators' 95% intervals are consistent with zero, and none reaches conventional significance.

The coefficient-comparison figure below shows exactly how wide each of those intervals is — that width is the honest bound on what this analysis can rule out.

Three ways of asking

One estimate agreeing with itself isn't evidence. Three designs that fail in different directions, so a real effect would have to hide from all of them at once.

Method 01

Two-way fixed effects

One regression with jurisdiction and year fixed effects, so each jurisdiction is compared against its own history and each year against that year's national average.

Why it can fail: under staggered adoption it can implicitly use already-treated states as part of the comparison group, biasing the average toward zero on its own — the exact problem this design exists to correct for.

Method 02

Sun & Abraham (2021)

Estimates a separate cohort-by-event-time effect for every adoption group, then averages them, avoiding the already-treated-as-control problem. Excludes Iowa (a treatment reversal) and always-treated states (no pre-period).

Why it can fail: it needs enough not-yet-treated states left in every period to serve as a clean comparison group; the estimates thin out at the edges of the panel.

Method 03

Callaway–Sant'Anna (2021)

Compares each adoption cohort only against states not yet treated at that time, then aggregates the resulting group-time effects into one overall estimate.

Why it can fail: it buys that clean comparison by discarding some comparisons outright, so its intervals are wider by construction.

One unstable cell

Weighting Sun-Abraham's interacted specification by raw ballot counts produces a near-singular design for at least one cohort×time cell (weight variance runs from a median of ~210 ballots to a statewide-aggregate row of ~3.4 million). The point estimate may still be informative; its standard error and p-value are not — don't read significance into that row (marked ** in the table) either way.

Limitations

  • Illinois and Massachusetts have confirmed signature-cure rights but a genuinely unknown adoption year after 8+ and 5+ independent research attempts — coded as pre-2016 by inference, treated as censored/unknown in the design, not a confirmed date.
  • Ohio and Montana are the two weakest-sourced rows in the 51-state treatment coding — not known to be wrong, just less independently corroborated than every other row.
  • Pennsylvania (no statewide law; county-optional cure) is coded untreated at the state level, a documented approximation, not a discovered fact. North Carolina's cure process came from litigation, not statute, and is excluded from the primary treatment variable by definition.
  • ACS demographic controls were deliberately deferred (no Census API key obtained); jurisdiction fixed effects absorb time-invariant covariates regardless.
  • The underlying treatment coding will go stale. Hundreds of election-related bills are enacted every year — this analysis reflects the 2014–2024 panel and coding as validated through several rounds of review, not a live-updating policy tracker.
  • County choropleth coverage: 7 states (Wisconsin and the New England states) report EAVS data at the town/municipality level, not county — shown gray on the map above, not mapped incorrectly or silently omitted.
